\chapter{Overview}\label{chap:overview}

\jpoints{} is a C program that finds the rational points of bounded height on the Jacobian of a
curve of genus~$2$, by sieving. This blueprint describes a Lean~4 development that verifies it: an
executable model of \jpoints{} in Lean, which follows the C code function by function; proofs that
the program of the model prints exactly the points of a short specification, in the order of the
specification, whatever choice of the sieve it makes; and a link from the model to the C code. This
chapter states the specification and the main theorems in words, each statement linked to the node
where it is stated precisely, with its Lean names; it says how the proofs go, what one has to trust
and what is not verified, and how the rest of the blueprint is organized.

\section{What \jpoints{} computes}\label{sec:overviewJpoints}

The input is a list $[f_0, f_1, \dots, f_6]$ of at most seven integers, constant term first, and a
height bound~$H$. The curve is
\[ C \colon y^2 = f(x)\,, \qquad f = f_0 + f_1 x + \dots + f_6 x^6\,, \]
with~$f$ squarefree of degree~$5$ or~$6$ ($f_6 = 0$ for a quintic), so that~$C$ has genus~$2$. Its
Jacobian~$J$ is an abelian surface over~$\QQ$; a point of~$J$ is the class of a divisor $(x_1, y_1)
+ (x_2, y_2) - D_\infty$, where~$D_\infty$ is the divisor of degree~$2$ at infinity. The quotient
of~$J$ by the map $P \mapsto -P$ is the Kummer surface, a quartic surface in~$\mathbb{P}^3$. In the
coordinates of Cassels and Flynn (\emph{Prolegomena to a Middlebrow Arithmetic of Curves of
Genus~$2$}, London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series~230, 1996), the class of $(x_1, y_1) +
(x_2, y_2) - D_\infty$ with~$x_1$, $x_2$ finite maps to $(1 : x_1 + x_2 : x_1 x_2 : d)$ for
some~$d$, the origin of~$J$ maps to $(0 : 0 : 0 : 1)$, and the Kummer surface has the equation
\[ K(a, b, c, d) = K_2(a, b, c)\, d^2 + K_1(a, b, c)\, d + K_0(a, b, c) = 0\,, \qquad
   K_2 = b^2 - 4ac\,, \]
with a cubic form~$K_1$ and a quartic form~$K_0$ in $a, b, c$ whose coefficients are linear and
quadratic in those of~$f$ (Definition~\ref{def:kummer}); it is the equation of Magma's
\texttt{KummerSurface}.

A rational point~$P$ of~$J$ gives a rational point of the Kummer surface, with integer coordinates
$(a, b, c, d)$ without common factor, unique up to sign. Not every rational point of the Kummer
surface comes from one of~$J$: whether it does is a condition that some integers are squares, in
three charts (Definition~\ref{def:Lifts}). For $a \ne 0$ the Mumford representation of the point is
$(x^2 - (b/a) x + c/a,\ v_1 x + v_0)$, and the two polynomials of the lifting test
(Definition~\ref{def:auxA2})
\begin{align*}
  A_2 &= a^3 d + f_2 a^4 + f_3 a^3 b + f_4 a^2 b^2 + f_5 a b (b^2 - a c) + f_6 (b^2 - a c)^2\,, \\
  B_2 &= a^2 c d + f_0 a^4 + f_4 a^2 c^2 + f_5 a b c^2 + f_6 b^2 c^2
\end{align*}
are $a^4 v_1^2$ and $a^4 v_0^2$; the point lifts when both are squares in~$\ZZ$, $0$ included. For
$a = 0 \ne b$ the divisor is a point at infinity plus the point with $x = c/b$, and the point lifts
when~$f_6$ and $F(c, b) = b^6 f(c/b)$ are squares, where $F(x, z) = \sum_k f_k x^k z^{6-k}$ is the
binary sextic form of~$f$; for $a = b = 0$ it lifts when~$f_6$ is a square.

The \emph{naive height} of a rational point~$P$ of~$J$ is $\max(|a|, |b|, |c|, |d|)$ for the coprime
integer coordinates of its image. For the height bound~$H$, \jpoints{} lists the points $(a, b, c,
d)$ of the Kummer surface that come from rational points of~$J$, normalized (the four coordinates
coprime, the first nonzero one of $a, b, c$ positive), with $|a|, |b|, |c| \le H$ and $|d| \le W$,
where $W = H$ unless the option \verb|-w| gives another bound. Without options these are the images
of the rational points of~$J$ of naive height at most~$H$, each pair $\pm P$ once, the origin left
out. Under the option \verb|-a| the height bound is on the image $(a : b : c)$ in the projective
plane, that is, on $|a|, |b|, |c|$ divided by $\gcd(a, b, c)$, and the fourth coordinate is bounded
only when \verb|-w| is given (Definition~\ref{def:InBounds}). For instance,
\verb|j-points '1 4 3 -8 2 8 7' 8 -q| lists the images of the rational points of naive height at
most~$8$ on the Jacobian of $y^2 = 7x^6 + 8x^5 + 2x^4 - 8x^3 + 3x^2 + 4x + 1$, one per line, as
\verb|(1, -2, 1, -6)|, \verb|(1, 0, 0, 1)|, \verb|(1, -1, 0, 4)|, \verb|(1, -1, 0, -8)| and
\verb|(7, 6, 3, -2)|. The first two are the images of the classes $2 (-1, \pm 3) - D_\infty$ and $2
(0, \pm 1) - D_\infty$, the next two those of $(0, 1) + (-1, \pm 3) - D_\infty$, and the last comes
from a pair of points conjugate over $\QQ(\sqrt{-3})$, whose $x$-coordinates are the roots of $7x^2
- 6x + 3$.

Over a triple $(a, b, c)$ the Kummer equation is a quadratic equation for~$d$. \jpoints{} runs
through the coprime triples of the box $|a|, |b|, |c| \le H$, the first nonzero coordinate positive;
over each, a rational root $d = n/m$ in lowest terms gives the candidate $(m a, m b, m c, n)$, which
it tests against the bounds and by the lifting test. Most triples carry no point, and between the
input and the points the C code does a good deal of work to rule them out cheaply; the chapters on
the model follow it. It reads and checks the command line and refuses a polynomial of degree
below~$5$ or not squarefree (\texttt{read\_input}). It replaces the curve by a monic quintic where
it can: a sextic with $f_0 = 0$ and $f_1 = \pm 1$ by its reversal, a quintic, given or so obtained,
with leading coefficient~$-1$ by $y^2 = f(-x)$ (\texttt{normalise\_curve}); it maps the points that
it finds back to the curve given. It isolates the real roots of~$f$ by Sturm sequences in exact
arithmetic and records the intervals on which~$f$ is negative (\texttt{real\_roots\_init}). For each
prime of a table it computes which classes modulo the prime the triples of points can have, from the
points of the Jacobian modulo the prime, and it sets up a condition at~$2$ modulo~$16$ or~$64$. A
cost model in doubles chooses the primes of the three stages of the sieve and its mode: the
\emph{box} of all triples; the \emph{real region}, which leaves out runs of triples $(a, b, c)$ for
which $a t^2 - b t + c$ has a real root at which~$f$ is negative; or, when the fourth coordinate is
bounded, the \emph{tube}, an analysis in doubles that leaves out the cells of the plane of $(b, c)$
whose triples have no real root~$d$ with $|d| \le W$ of their Kummer equation, possibly cut to the
real region. It searches first the points over $(0, 0, 1)$ and over the triples $(s^2, 2st, t^2)$ of
the double points, the latter with a sieve of their own, and then the other triples: for each first
coordinate~$a$ the rows $(a, b)$ that the sieving primes dividing~$a$ and the condition at~$2$
leave, and in each row the third coordinates~$c$, as the bits of arrays of~$64$-bit words that the
tables of the first two stages, the tests of the third stage and the test for common factors thin
out, in the tube and the real region only within the ranges of words of the row. The survivors go to
the exact check, which solves the Kummer equation, applies the bounds and the lifting test, and
prints each point in the format of the option \verb|-f|. Under \verb|-1| the program stops at the
first point; without \verb|-q| it prints a report around the points.

The C code is that of \jpoints{}~3.0.1, from
\href{https://github.com/MichaelStollBayreuth/j-points}{its repository on GitHub}, whose
\href{https://github.com/MichaelStollBayreuth/j-points/blob/\jpointscommit/README.md}{\texttt{README.md}}
describes the program and its options. Every line number in this blueprint refers to one commit of
the repository, the one that the record of the correspondence pins (Chapter~\ref{chap:ccode}); the
link to the description points to that commit too.

\section{The four parts}\label{sec:overviewParts}

The development has four parts, in separate folders. The specification and the model import neither
each other nor the proofs, and the proofs import both; \verb|scripts/check_layout.py| checks the
imports.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \emph{specification} (\verb|JPoints/Spec|) states what \jpoints{} computes: the Kummer
    equation, the lifting test, the normalized points within the bounds of the search, Sturm's
    theorem as a proposition, and the list of the points in the order in which \jpoints{} finds
    them. It consists of two short files that use Mathlib and nothing of the model, so that they can
    be read on their own (Chapter~\ref{chap:spec}).
  \item The \emph{model} (\verb|JPoints/Model|) is an executable version of \jpoints{} in Lean. It
    mirrors the C code function by function: the same loops, tables, bit arrays of~$64$-bit words
    and index arithmetic, and the doubles that decide what is searched as their IEEE~754 bits. It is
    plain code on Lean core and \verb|Std|, without Mathlib and without contracts, and forms a
    library of its own; the program \verb|j-points-model| (\verb|ModelMain.lean|) runs it and has
    the command line of \jpoints{}. The model also holds a brute-force reference, which tests every
    triple of the box by the exact check, run as the program \verb|j-points-brute|
    (\verb|BruteMain.lean|). Chapters~\ref{chap:model} to~\ref{chap:region} present the model part
    by part, each component with its contract.
  \item The \emph{proofs} (\verb|JPoints/Proof|) show that the model meets the specification. Each
    component of the model has a contract, and the main theorems, in \verb|JPoints/Proof/Main.lean|,
    put the contracts together (Chapter~\ref{chap:theorems}). Statements of general interest that
    Mathlib lacks, on roots of polynomials, Sturm sequences, lists and the like, are stated at
    Mathlib's generality in \verb|JPoints/Mathlib|.
  \item The \emph{link to the C code} (Chapter~\ref{chap:ccode}): a record of which Lean declaration
    models which lines of C, kept in the paragraphs \verb|C:| of the docstrings of the model and,
    for the pin, the excluded lines and the lock, in the folder \verb|correspondence|, checked
    against the C code at the pinned commit; and differential tests that run \verb|j-points-model|
    and \jpoints{} on the same command lines and compare what they print.
\end{enumerate}
The model does without Mathlib so that the executable and the differential tests depend neither on
Mathlib nor on the proofs. The specification does without the model so that one can read what the
theorems claim without reading how \jpoints{} works. The folder \verb|comparator| holds the
certification of the main theorems (Section~\ref{sec:overviewTrust}).

\section{The specification}\label{sec:overviewSpec}

The specification is the one part whose correctness no proof can check. Wherever there was a choice,
it therefore says what the C code computes, and Chapter~\ref{chap:spec} states it in full.

\emph{Points.} For the coefficient list of~$f$, the height bound~$H$, the option \verb|-a| and the
bound~$W$ of \verb|-w|, if given, a quadruple $(a, b, c, d)$ of integers is a point
(Definition~\ref{def:IsPoint}) when
\begin{enumerate}
  \item it lies on the Kummer surface, $K(a, b, c, d) = 0$ (Definition~\ref{def:kummer});
  \item it comes from a rational point of the Jacobian, by the lifting test of
    Section~\ref{sec:overviewJpoints} (Definition~\ref{def:Lifts});
  \item it is normalized: $\gcd(a, b, c, d) = 1$, and $a > 0$, or $a = 0 < b$, or $a = b = 0 < c$
    (Definition~\ref{def:IsNormalized}), which leaves out the origin $(0, 0, 0, 1)$;
  \item it lies within the bounds of the search: without \verb|-a|, $|a|, |b|, |c| \le H$ and $|d|
    \le W$, with $W = H$ when \verb|-w| is not given; under \verb|-a|, $|a|, |b|, |c| \le H \gcd(a,
    b, c)$, and $|d| \le W$ only when \verb|-w| is given (Definition~\ref{def:InBounds}).
\end{enumerate}

\emph{The order.} The list of the points, \lname{JPoints.orderedPoints}
(Definition~\ref{def:orderedPoints}), is in the order in which \jpoints{} prints them. It is formed
on the curve searched (Definition~\ref{def:searchedCoeffs}): the coprime triples $(a, b, c)$ of the
box, the first nonzero coordinate positive, those on the conic $b^2 = 4ac$ first, that is $(0, 0,
1)$ and the triples $(s^2, 2st, t^2)$ of the double points, then the others, each in lexicographic
order (Definition~\ref{def:orderedTriples}); over each triple the rational roots of the Kummer
equation in a fixed order, the root $(\sqrt{\Delta} - K_1)/(2K_2)$ first
(Definition~\ref{def:kummerRoots}); the candidates over them that are points; and each point taken
back to the curve given (Definition~\ref{def:backPoint}). The sieve leaves out only triples over
which no point lies, so this order is the same in every mode of the sieve, and the specification can
fix it.

\emph{Squarefreeness.} \jpoints{} refuses a polynomial that is not squarefree over~$\QQ$; the
specification has the polynomial of the coefficient list over~$\QQ$ (Definition~\ref{def:poly}), and
squarefreeness is Mathlib's. The statements on membership hold for the curves that the program does
not refuse: at most seven coefficients, degree at least~$5$, and a squarefree polynomial. For these
the Kummer equation over a nonzero triple is never constant zero (Lemma~\ref{lem:kummerK0_ne_zero}),
so that at most two points lie over each.

\emph{Sturm's theorem} (Definition~\ref{def:SturmTheorem}): for a squarefree real polynomial~$p$ and
reals $u \le v$, the number of roots of~$p$ in $(u, v]$ plus the number of sign variations of the
Sturm sequence of~$p$ and~$p'$ at~$v$ is that number at~$u$. Mathlib has the Sturm sequence and the
sign variations of a list, but not the theorem; it is a proposition, assumed by the theorems that
need it (Section~\ref{sec:overviewTheorems}).

\emph{The brute-force reference} (Definition~\ref{def:bruteForce}) is a plain program of the model
that runs through the coprime triples of the box and checks, on the curve given, the candidates over
each by the bounds and the lifting test of \jpoints{}. It lists exactly the points
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mem_bruteForce_iff}), each once (Theorem~\ref{thm:nodup_bruteForce}), in the order
of the search (Theorem~\ref{thm:bruteForce_eq_filter_flatMap_kummerPoints}), and the list of the
specification is its list of the curve searched, taken back to the curve given
(Theorem~\ref{thm:orderedPoints_eq_map_bruteForce}). So the specification can be tested: as
\verb|j-points-brute| the reference agrees with the lists of points that Magma's Kummer package
computed independently for the test curves of the C repository, and with \jpoints{}
(\verb|scripts/brutetest.py|).

\section{The main theorems}\label{sec:overviewTheorems}

\emph{The points of the specification} are what one expects them to be: for every curve that the
program does not refuse, the list of the specification holds exactly the points of the curve given
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mem_orderedPoints_iff}), and for every curve it has no duplicates
(Theorem~\ref{thm:nodup_orderedPoints}).

\emph{The main theorem} (Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.run_eq_of_readInput_eq_ok_of_quiet}) is about the
program of the model, \lname{JPoints.Program.run} (Definition~\ref{def:Program.run}), a function
from the command line to the text that it prints and its exit status. On a command line that
\texttt{read\_input} accepts (Definition~\ref{def:Cli.readInput}), under \verb|-q| and with a format
of \verb|-f| that the model admits (Definition~\ref{def:Printf.parseFormat}), the program prints the
points of the specification for the coefficients, the height bound, \verb|-a| and \verb|-w| of the
command line, in their order, only the first under \verb|-1| (Definition~\ref{def:firstIf}), each in
the format (Definition~\ref{def:pointText}: by \texttt{printf} when its coordinates fit the machine
words of the exact check, by \texttt{gmp\_printf} otherwise), and exits with the status~$0$.

\emph{The run without} \verb|-q|
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.exists_run_eq_of_readInput_eq_ok_of_quiet_eq_false}): the program prints
the same points inside its report, after the head of the report, the message of
\texttt{normalise\_curve} and the text of the choice of the sieve, and before the numbers of the
survivors of the stages and of the points found, for some totals of its counters, and exits with the
status~$0$.

\emph{The points read back.} Under the default format \verb|(%ld, %ld, %ld, %ld)\n|
(Definition~\ref{def:defaultPointText}), a list of points whose texts make up what the program
prints under \verb|-q| is the list of the specification, only the first under \verb|-1|
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.eq_firstIf_orderedPoints_of_run_eq}); without \verb|-1| it holds exactly
the points of the curve given (Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.mem_iff_isPoint_of_run_eq}), and in any case
each of them once (Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.nodup_of_run_eq}).

These theorems hold for every curve that the program accepts, for every value of the options of the
sieve and whatever primes and mode the cost model chooses. Besides the command line accepted
(\verb|hi|), the flag \verb|-q| (\verb|hq|) and the format (\verb|hf|), the theorems on the program
have two hypotheses, stated in the terms of the globals \verb|input| that \texttt{read\_input} sets
(Definition~\ref{def:Cli.Input}).

\emph{Sturm's theorem}, \verb|hS : input.sievePrimes3 = 0 ∨ SturmTheorem|. The real region, and the
tube when it is cut to the real region, rest on the intervals on which~$f$ is negative, which
\texttt{real\_roots\_init} derives from isolating intervals of the real roots of~$f$, found by
counting sign variations of Sturm sequences (Lemma~\ref{lem:realRootsInit_spec}); the counts are
those of the roots by Sturm's theorem. Since the cost model, whose values the proofs do not read,
chooses the mode of the sieve, the theorems assume Sturm's theorem whenever the sieve has primes.
They do not assume it when the sieve has no primes at all, that is when \verb|input.sievePrimes3|,
the number of the primes of its three stages together, is~$0$, as \verb|-N| with a value of at
most~$0$ makes it: the program then searches the box, which needs neither the tube nor the real
region.

\emph{The height bound}, \verb|hH : input.height ≤ 2 ^ 26|. In the real region, \texttt{row\_region}
computes for each row $(a, b)$ the ends of the ranges of~$c$ that it leaves out, the values $B x - A
x x$ of $q(x) = b x - a x^2$ at the ends~$x$ of the intervals on which~$f$ is negative, in doubles,
with~$A$ and~$B$ the doubles of~$a$ and~$b$, moved inwards by a margin. Near $x = b/a$ the two
products nearly cancel. A height bound of at most~$2^{26}$ keeps them below~$2^{53}$, where each of
the three roundings errs by at most~$1/2$, the computed end lies within~$7/4$ of~$q(x)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:abs_sub_le_of_roundings}), which the margin covers
(Lemma~\ref{lem:Double.value_lt_of_marginLo}), and no range left out holds the word of a point
(Lemma~\ref{lem:NegativeIntervals.Sound.not_regionExcludes_of_hasPointOver}). At larger height
bounds the products can pass~$2^{53}$, the computed ends can lose their margins, and
\jpoints{}~3.0.1 can drop words of the real region that hold points, and miss these points. The tube
needs no such bound: its analysis is sound for every value of \texttt{TUBE\_EPS} between $2^{-47}$
(Definition~\ref{def:tubeEpsMin}) and~$1$.

\formalnote{The model refuses, with the exit status~$11$ and before it prints anything else, a
format of \texttt{-f} with conversions other than \texttt{\%ld} (with flags, width and precision)
and \texttt{\%\%}, or with more than four conversions, where \jpoints{} passes any format to
\texttt{printf}; so the theorems on the program assume a format that the model admits. The program
of the model returns its text instead of writing it, and on a closed pipe it exits quietly with the
status~$141$ that a shell reports for the signal that ends \jpoints{}.}

\section{How the proofs go}\label{sec:overviewProofs}

The specification is a filter of an enumeration: over the triples of the search in turn, the
candidates that are points. The program runs through the same triples in the same order, in three
parts (Lemma~\ref{lem:flatMap_orderedTriples}, Definition~\ref{def:mainTriples}): the triple $(0, 0,
1)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:findPointsAtInfinity_spec}), the triples of the double points
(Lemma~\ref{lem:findDoublePoints_spec_of_doubleSetup}), and the other triples, by first coordinate,
by row and by third coordinate. It leaves most triples out by tests and checks the others exactly.
Three facts show that it prints exactly the points of the specification.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The exact check is exact}: over a triple that it is handed, \texttt{check\_one\_point}
    prints exactly the candidates that are points, in the order of the roots of the Kummer equation
    (Lemma~\ref{lem:checkOnePoint_spec}).
  \item \emph{The tests are sound}: each keeps every triple over which a point lies, a candidate
    that comes from a rational point of the Jacobian, whatever the bounds
    (Definition~\ref{def:HasPointOver}). The tests are Boolean functions in the terms of the
    specification: of the units of work (Definition~\ref{def:unitKeeps}), of the rows
    (Definition~\ref{def:rowKeeps}), of the third coordinates, by the three stages, the mask of the
    row, the ends of the row and the common factors (Definition~\ref{def:colKeeps}), and of the
    bands (Definition~\ref{def:bandKeeps}); each has its soundness lemma
    (Lemmas~\ref{lem:unitKeeps_of_hasPointOver}, \ref{lem:rowKeeps_of_hasPointOver},
    \ref{lem:colKeeps_of_hasPointOver} and~\ref{lem:bandKeeps_of_points_ne_nil}). With the exact
    check, the points that the sieve of a row finds (Definition~\ref{def:rowPoints}) make up, over
    the rows of a first coordinate, the points of the specification over its triples
    (Lemma~\ref{lem:flatMap_rowPoints_rowsFrom}), and the units of work hold the points of the main
    search (Lemma~\ref{lem:flatMap_range_unitTriples_unitA}).
  \item \emph{The text is that of the points of the curve given}: what the exact check prints for a
    point of the curve searched is the text of the point taken back to the curve given
    (Lemma~\ref{lem:CheckGlobals.text_eq_pointText}), and under \verb|-1| the program stops after
    the first (Lemma~\ref{lem:textOf_firstIf_flatMap_orderedTriples}).
\end{enumerate}

The soundness of the tests is the mathematics of the sieve. A point that comes from a rational point
of the Jacobian reduces modulo every odd prime to a class that the tables admit
(Lemma~\ref{lem:refAdmits12_of_lifts}, Chapter~\ref{chap:sieve-numbers}) and satisfies the condition
at~$2$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:twoadicAdmissible_of_hasPointOver}); for a monic quintic its first coordinate
is a square (Lemma~\ref{lem:isSquare_of_mem_kummerPoints}), and over a double point $(s^2, 2st,
t^2)$ the value $F(t, s)$ is a square (Lemma~\ref{lem:isSquare_sexticForm_of_hasPointOver}). The
sieve of a row applies these tests on~$64$-bit words, and its stages are shown to drop exactly the
bits of the classes that the tables do not admit (Chapter~\ref{chap:words}). In the tube, a point
with $|d| \le W$ gives a root of the Kummer quadratic of its triple within the bound
(Lemma~\ref{lem:CheckGlobals.WF.tubeRoot_of_points_ne_nil}), and a cell that the analysis rules out
holds no such root (Lemma~\ref{lem:TubeSetup.not_root_of_tubeCellAnalyzeGen},
Chapter~\ref{chap:tube}). In the real region, $f(x) \ge 0$ at the real roots~$x$ of $a t^2 - b t +
c$ over a point (Lemma~\ref{lem:sum_nonneg_of_hasPointOver}), and~$f$ is negative inside the
intervals that \texttt{real\_roots\_init} records; a word that \texttt{row\_region} leaves out
would, by the intermediate value theorem, give such a root inside one of them, so it leaves out the
word of no point (Chapter~\ref{chap:region}).

The model's side is a contract for each component. A component is a function in Lean's \verb|Id|
monad that follows one C function; its contract is a triple $\{P\}\ \mathit{comp}\ \{Q\}$ of the
program logic of Lean core, proved by its tactic \verb|vcgen|, with the invariants of the loops as
named definitions. The contracts compose, from the sieve of a row (Lemma~\ref{lem:sift_spec}), the
step of the rows (Lemma~\ref{lem:run_rowStep}) and the units of work (Lemma~\ref{lem:runA_spec}) up
to \texttt{find\_points} (Lemma~\ref{lem:findPoints_spec}) and the search of \texttt{main}
(Lemma~\ref{lem:Program.mainSearch_spec}); with the contract of \texttt{read\_input}
(Lemma~\ref{lem:Cli.readInput_spec}) they give the theorems on the program. The proofs hold for
every choice of the sieve: a well-formed choice (Definition~\ref{def:IsChoice}) says what the search
reads after the choice, and \texttt{init\_fmodpsquare} makes one whatever values the floats of its
cost model take (Lemma~\ref{lem:initFmodpsquare_isChoice}). The operations on the doubles that
decide what is searched round to nearest (Definition~\ref{def:Double.IsRounding},
Lemma~\ref{lem:Double.isRounding_add}), and an error calculus on top bounds the errors of the tube
and of \texttt{row\_region} (Chapter~\ref{chap:doubles}). The compile-time constants of the C code
are definitions of the model; the proofs use only some facts about their values, decided next to
them, and the constants check builds the model and the proofs with other values of the constants,
each meeting these facts (Chapter~\ref{chap:ccode}).

\section{What one has to trust}\label{sec:overviewTrust}

\emph{The specification.} Its two files (Chapter~\ref{chap:spec}) are what the theorems mean. The
statements on the program also use definitions on top of them: Sturm's theorem
(Definition~\ref{def:SturmTheorem}), the first point only under \verb|-1|
(Definition~\ref{def:firstIf}), the texts of a point under an admitted format and under the
default format (Definitions~\ref{def:pointText} and~\ref{def:defaultPointText}), and the bound on
the fourth coordinate and the text of a point for the globals of the command line
(Definition~\ref{def:Cli.Input.pointText}). The statements also
name definitions of the model: the program (Definition~\ref{def:Program.run}), \texttt{read\_input}
and its globals (Definitions~\ref{def:Cli.readInput} and~\ref{def:Cli.Input}), the formats of
\verb|-f| (Definition~\ref{def:Printf.parseFormat}) and, through the texts, the model of
\texttt{printf} and \texttt{gmp\_printf}; and the brute-force reference
(Definition~\ref{def:bruteForce}).

\emph{The kernel and comparator.} No theorem has a \verb|sorry|, and every one depends on the axioms
\verb|propext|, \verb|Quot.sound| and \verb|Classical.choice| only; \verb|native_decide|,
\verb|bv_decide| and their kin, which would add axioms, occur nowhere, which
\verb|scripts/check_layout.py| checks. Comparator, a judge of Lean proofs built into Lake as
\verb|lake comparator|, certifies ten statements: the four on the brute-force reference
(Theorems~\ref{thm:mem_bruteForce_iff}, \ref{thm:nodup_bruteForce},
\ref{thm:bruteForce_eq_filter_flatMap_kummerPoints} and~\ref{thm:orderedPoints_eq_map_bruteForce}),
the two on the points of the specification (Theorems~\ref{thm:mem_orderedPoints_iff}
and~\ref{thm:nodup_orderedPoints}), the main theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.run_eq_of_readInput_eq_ok_of_quiet}) and the three on the points read back
(Theorems~\ref{thm:Program.eq_firstIf_orderedPoints_of_run_eq},
\ref{thm:Program.mem_iff_isPoint_of_run_eq} and~\ref{thm:Program.nodup_of_run_eq}). Its challenge,
in the folder \verb|comparator|, imports only Mathlib and the model, restates the specification and
the four definitions on top of it verbatim, and states the ten theorems; comparator checks that the
library proves the same statements, comparing the full bodies of the definitions that they use, with
no axioms but the three above. On 7~November~2026 \verb|lake comparator --paranoid| accepted them,
with Lean's kernel and the five external checkers \verb|leanchecker-paranoid|, \verb|lean4lean|,
\verb|nanoda|, \verb|con-leche| and \verb|con-ron|. The run without \verb|-q|
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Program.exists_run_eq_of_readInput_eq_ok_of_quiet_eq_false}) is proved but not
certified.

\emph{The executable.} The theorems are about the Lean definitions of the model. One trusts that the
program \verb|j-points-model| writes the text of the program of the model
(Definition~\ref{def:Program.run}) and returns its exit status, and that Lean's compiler and runtime
run the definitions as they are. The model has no \verb|partial|, \verb|unsafe|,
\verb|implemented_by| or \verb|extern|, which \verb|scripts/check_layout.py| checks, so the compiled
code is that of the definitions that the proofs see.

\emph{Sturm's theorem} is a hypothesis of the theorems on the program, not an axiom: a proof of
\lname{JPoints.SturmTheorem} in Lean would make them hold without it, and the runs without sieving
primes do not need it.

\emph{The cost model.} Its floats are Lean core's \verb|Float|, whose arithmetic and \texttt{log}
and \texttt{pow} are those of the platform, as in \jpoints{}. No proof reads their values: the
theorems hold for every value that they take, hence whatever the C library of a machine returns.
What the program prints under \verb|-q| does not depend on them.

\section{What is not verified}\label{sec:overviewNot}

\emph{The C code itself.} Lean has no semantics of C, and no proof relates the model to the C code.
The model is a faithful translation, and two things link it to the C code
(Chapter~\ref{chap:ccode}). The record of the correspondence covers every line of code of
\verb|j-points.c|, \verb|j-sift.c| and \verb|j-points.h|: the lines that the model covers, named in
the docstrings of its declarations, the others excluded from the model, each with its kind and
reason, all checked against the C code at the pinned commit (the table of the record is
Appendix~\ref{chap:correspondence}). The differential tests compare \verb|j-points-model| with
\jpoints{}, built at the pin, byte for byte (the standard output, the standard error and the exit
status), on the suites of the C repository, random curves, the options, the errors, random command
lines and failed writes.

\emph{What the model leaves out.} The threads of the option \verb|-t|: the model follows the
sequential path of the C code, and that \jpoints{} prints with threads what it prints with one is
not proved (the differential tests compare runs with threads too). The management of the memory and
of the GMP variables, and the instrumentation under \verb|DEBUG| and \verb|VERBOSE|.

\emph{The other builds.} The model is the default build of \jpoints{} on x86-64 with LP64, glibc and
GMP; the vector builds of the sieve (\verb|VECTOR=avx2| or \verb|sse2|) are not modeled.

\emph{The platform.} The model rounds each operation on doubles as IEEE~754 prescribes, as the code
that gcc makes of the default build does on x86-64, and prints as glibc's \texttt{printf} and GMP's
\texttt{gmp\_printf} print; its sort of the rates of the primes follows glibc's \texttt{qsort}. That
the platform does the same is checked by the differential tests, not proved; of the sort, the proofs
use only that it permutes the entries (Lemma~\ref{lem:toList_qsort_perm}). The values of
\texttt{log} and \texttt{pow} in the cost model are those of the platform, in the model as in
\jpoints{}: the messages without \verb|-q| that name the chosen primes and the mode depend on them,
and the differential tests compare them on one machine.

\emph{The counts of the report.} Without \verb|-q| the theorem on the report says only that the
numbers of the survivors of the stages and of the points found are printed for some totals of the
counters; that they are the totals that the C code counts is checked by the differential tests.

\section{How to read this blueprint}\label{sec:overviewReading}

Chapter~\ref{chap:spec} states the specification and the brute-force reference, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:theorems} the main theorems with their proof from the contracts: the order of the
search, the tests and their soundness, the exact check and the text of the points.
Chapter~\ref{chap:model} says how the C code is modeled and covers the input and the output: the
command line, the exact check, \texttt{printf}, the curve searched and the program. The following
chapters take the sieve part by part: the classes of points modulo primes, the condition at~$2$, the
tables and the choice of the sieve (Chapter~\ref{chap:sieve-numbers}); the sieve on words, the step
of the rows and the double points (Chapter~\ref{chap:words}); the doubles and the real roots of~$f$
(Chapter~\ref{chap:doubles}); the tube (Chapter~\ref{chap:tube}); the real region and the bands
(Chapter~\ref{chap:region}). Chapter~\ref{chap:ccode} describes the link to the C code, and
Appendix~\ref{chap:correspondence} is the table of the record, generated from it.

\emph{Notation.} The coefficient of~$x^k$ in~$f$ is~$f_k$, for $k = 0, \dots, 6$, and $F(x, z) = z^6
f(x/z)$ is the binary sextic form. Points are quadruples $(a, b, c, d)$ as the program prints them,
triples $(a, b, c)$ their first three coordinates. Lean names are in typewriter, like
\lname{JPoints.IsPoint}, and so are the names of C functions, macros and options, like
\texttt{row\_region}, \texttt{TUBE\_EPS} and \verb|-w|.

\emph{Nodes.} Each definition, lemma and theorem of the blueprint names the Lean declarations that
state it, and its statement says in words what they say; every one is formalized and proved, without
\verb|sorry|. A remark headed \emph{Formalization} marks a place where the formal statement or the
model departs from the C code or from what a reader would expect.

\emph{The dependency graph.} In the web version, the graph has a node for each definition (a box)
and for each lemma and theorem (an ellipse), and an edge from a node to each node whose statement
(dashed) or proof (solid) uses it; green marks what is formalized.

\emph{Links to the C code.} A reference like \texttt{j-points.c:100-120} links to these lines of the
C code at the pinned commit. Inside a node that names declarations of the model, each such link is a
range that their docstrings record, and \verb|scripts/correspondence.py| checks the links together
with the record.
